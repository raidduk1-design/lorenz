A weather model, three lines long

In 1961–62, MIT meteorologist Edward Lorenz reduced a convection cell — warm fluid rising, cooling, sinking, the engine of weather — to three variables: x, the intensity of the convection; y, a temperature contrast between the ascending and descending currents; and z, how far the vertical temperature profile departs from linear. The result is the system above: a simplified model of convection, and one of the most studied objects in all of mathematics. The discovery itself came with his earlier, twelve-variable convection model: he interrupted one computation, re-entered the numbers rounded to three decimals, and the “forecast” came out completely different. Two years later he had distilled that behavior into the three equations above.

A nudge of 10⁻⁸

The two dots are identical forecasts; the amber one starts 10⁻⁸ farther from the origin (x = 1.00000001). For a dozen time-units they fly wing to wing. Then one loops slightly higher, and the error runs away: 10⁻⁸ becomes 1.0 in about 28 units on the classical system. t* is this pair's first crossing of a chosen error threshold: it shifts with the threshold, the starting pair, and even the integrator's step size — it is not a universal prediction deadline. This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions — the butterfly effect. The punchline is not that weather is random, but that point forecasts from finite-precision data lose their edge after a few time-units. That says how fast individual trajectories separate; it says little about probabilistic forecasts or the attractor's statistics, which the system retains.

A shape between dimensions

One trajectory never repeats and never wanders off: it lives on a strange attractor — a sheet folded into a fractal whose dimensions sit slightly above 2. The slope of our box-counting curve (≈ 1.6–1.9) is a finite-sample estimate over the scales we count, not the attractor's dimension — counting boxes on a finite orbit undershoots the true value. The established figures are higher: the Kaplan–Yorke dimension of the classical system is ≈ 2.06, and correlation-dimension estimates also lie just above 2 (≈ 2.05). Not a curve, not an ordinary surface either. And λ₁ ≈ 0.9 says a tiny error doubles every ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.8 time-units. The same mathematics now appears in turbulence, climate, neurons, the heart and lasers.
